Resume Skills Guide

How to choose skills for your resume—and prove them

A useful Skills section is not the longest list you can fit on the page. It is a compact map of relevant capabilities that the rest of your resume can support with projects, experience, education or certifications.

Quick rule: prioritise skills that are relevant to the target job, true for you, and supported somewhere else in the resume. A shorter list with evidence is usually more credible than a wall of keywords.

Different kinds of skills

Job descriptions mix several types of requirements. Separating them helps you decide what belongs in the Skills section and what belongs in your experience bullets.

  • Tools and technologies: Excel, SQL, Python, Figma, Salesforce, AutoCAD, SAP, Java, Power BI.
  • Methods and practices: financial modelling, user research, test automation, SEO, forecasting, stakeholder interviews.
  • Domain knowledge: GST operations, B2B SaaS, retail inventory, healthcare claims, supply chain planning.
  • Transferable capabilities: analysis, facilitation, negotiation, project coordination, written communication.

Soft skills such as “communication” or “leadership” can matter, but a plain list provides little evidence. If leadership is important to the job, show a bullet where you led a project, coordinated people, handled an escalation or made a decision.

Start with the target job description

Read the role for recurring tools, responsibilities and outcomes. Separate requirements into three groups: skills you already use confidently, skills you have used but only at a basic level, and skills you do not have. Only the first two groups should appear on your resume, and the wording should match your real level.

Example: A data analyst job asks for SQL, Excel, Power BI, stakeholder communication and Python. If you have strong SQL/Excel/Power BI projects but only completed one introductory Python course, do not present all five skills as equal “expertise”. You can list Python honestly as basic familiarity or omit it if space is limited.

Turn important skills into evidence

When a skill matters to the job, the strongest place for it is often a project or work bullet where the reader can see what you did with it. The Skills section then acts as a fast index.

Keyword only: SQL
Evidence: “Wrote SQL queries across sales and customer tables to create a weekly retention report and reduce manual spreadsheet consolidation.”
Keyword only: Stakeholder management
Evidence: “Ran weekly requirement reviews with sales and operations teams, documented priority changes and coordinated release handoffs.”

Evidence does not need a dramatic metric every time. Scope, complexity, frequency, ownership and context are all useful forms of proof.

How to group a Skills section

A grouped Skills section is easier to scan than a single long comma-separated list. Use categories only when they help the reader.

Data: SQL, Excel, Power BI, Tableau
Programming: Python, Pandas
Workflow: Git, Jira
Business: KPI reporting, requirements analysis

Do not create ten categories with one item each. For many resumes, two to four groups are enough.

Should you show skill levels?

Labels such as “Beginner / Intermediate / Advanced” can be useful when they are honest, but they are subjective. Visual progress bars such as “Excel 90%” often create false precision. A better signal is the depth of the evidence: number of projects, complexity of work, professional use, certification where relevant, or the kind of task you completed.

If a job requires a tool you have only studied, you can write “basic” or “coursework” where appropriate rather than implying professional experience.

Skills for freshers

Freshers should avoid filling the page with every technology encountered in college. Choose the tools and capabilities that appear in your strongest projects or internships. If Java is listed, be ready to explain what you built. If Excel is listed, be ready to discuss formulas, pivots, lookups or analysis you actually performed.

Non-technical freshers can also provide evidence. A campus event can show vendor coordination, budgeting and outreach. A research assignment can show survey design, analysis and presentation. A volunteer role can show operations or communication.

Examples by role

Software engineering

Languages, frameworks, databases, APIs, testing, version control, cloud/deployment tools and relevant architecture concepts. Do not list technologies you cannot use without substantial help.

Data and analytics

SQL, spreadsheets, BI tools, Python/R where used, data cleaning, visualisation, statistics and business reporting. Put analytical outcomes in projects or experience.

Sales

Prospecting, discovery, CRM tools, pipeline management, proposal development, negotiation and account management. Evidence can include territory, customer segment, deal process or retention/expansion work.

Marketing

Content planning, paid media platforms, SEO, analytics, email tools, research and campaign reporting. Separate tools from strategy and show campaigns or deliverables.

Finance and operations

Excel, ERP/accounting systems, reconciliation, forecasting, reporting, process documentation and compliance knowledge where relevant.

Skills to avoid or reconsider

  • Skills copied from the vacancy that you have never used.
  • Very broad traits such as “hardworking” with no evidence.
  • Outdated tools that are not relevant to the role and consume space.
  • Basic universal actions such as “internet browsing” unless the job genuinely requires a specialised version.
  • Duplicated synonyms added only to increase keyword count.

Skills and ATS matching

Applicant tracking systems differ, and no public checker can reproduce every employer's private configuration. Still, using the normal industry term for a real skill can help both software and humans understand relevance. If a posting uses “Power BI” and you genuinely use Power BI, write the standard name rather than an unusual abbreviation.

Do not treat an ATS checker as permission to invent missing skills. Use missing terms as a review list: if you have the experience but described it differently, clarify the wording. If you do not have the skill, leave it out and decide whether the gap matters enough to learn for future applications.

Skills checklist

  • Every listed skill is true and interview-defensible.
  • The most important job-relevant skills are easy to find.
  • Critical skills have evidence in projects, experience or education.
  • Categories are useful, not decorative.
  • Levels are not exaggerated or expressed as meaningless percentages.
  • The section does not crowd out stronger evidence.
